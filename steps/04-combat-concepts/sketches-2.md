# Step 4 sketches: second pass

Oct 16, 2026. Started from scratch after your notes on the first pass, without the schema. Here are three ideas for what a fight is. Each is built around one thing I think would be fun to build a party for and fun to watch. They're different games, not settings of one game, so they don't line up in a table. Names, units and numbers are placeholders.

- **Grudges.** Every enemy holds a grudge against whoever bothers it, and the fight is a running struggle over whom it's after. You're a raid leader: you build a team that controls a monster's attention, and during the fight you call how hard to push.
- **Hearts.** Your units are characters who decide for themselves, each with a temperament and a measure of wits. You're their coach: you set each one's attitude for long stretches, and they interpret it.
- **The Ladder.** Every hit knocks footing out from under its target, and a foe that loses its footing can be staggered, knocked down and crushed. You're an engineer: you build a party that climbs the ladder, then watch the machine run. You have no input during the fight.

What I need from you is the same as before: react to whole systems. Pick one, blend them, or throw them out.

## Grudges

**The idea.** Every enemy keeps a tally of how much each of your units has bothered it. Hurting it adds to that unit's grudge. Healing someone it's fighting adds to the healer's. A taunt adds a lot at once. It goes after whoever it holds the biggest grudge against, most of the time, and the bigger the lead, the surer. At the start of a fight nobody has a grudge yet, so blows land at random: every fight opens in chaos and settles as grudges form. The screen always says whom each enemy is glaring at. That's the spine of every fight: who it's after, and whether that changes.

**How a fight runs.** Rounds: everyone acts once a round, fastest first, and only exact ties are random. Your units fight back the same way. Each goes after whatever has hurt it most, and one that hasn't been hurt picks at random, so your attacks spread out naturally. A unit's card can give it its own habit, such as an archer who picks off the wounded or a witch-hunter who goes for casters. A hit on an enemy that's glaring at someone else is a **blindside**, and does half again as much. So a tank who holds the boss's eye turns everyone else's hits into blindsides.

**Stats.** Seven, each with one job:

- **Health.**
- **Might:** damage with weapons.
- **Will:** damage with spells, and healing. A battle priest stretches one stat both ways.
- **Guard:** taken off every weapon hit. Spells ignore it, so a thick-hided enemy calls for spells, blindsides or armour-breaking moves.
- **Speed:** who acts first in a round.
- **Presence:** how much grudge everything you do stirs up. Tanks have lots, and cutpurses almost none. A storm witch with high Presence and a warding spell is a caster who tanks.
- **MP:** pays for spells and prayers, and lasts the whole run, not one fight.

Crits and dodges aren't stats. Blindsides are the reliable bonus, and a few moves and pieces of gear carry a crit or dodge chance written on them.

**Your hand in it: the call.** Steady, Even or All-out, for the whole party, changed whenever you like. Each unit takes it its own way. On All-out, strikers hit harder and stir up more grudge, healers cast big, expensive heals, and the tank swings instead of bracing. On Steady, strikers ease off so they don't pull the boss off the tank, healers use small, cheap heals, and the tank braces. Two things pull against each other. Pushing risks the boss turning on someone fragile, and pushing burns MP the boss fight will need. So the right call keeps changing: it's a raid leader's read of "is the tank holding, and can we afford this?"

**Luck.** The opening chaos before grudges form, how surely an enemy follows its grudge, which move an enemy picks, and the few chances written on moves and gear. A party that catches the boss's eye fast and holds it by a wide margin takes most of the luck out.

**How it ends.** Harm is free and healing costs MP, on both sides. A long fight drains the healers, so it always tips one way. An enemy shaman's power runs out too, and the text says so.

**Reading it.** Glares ("The ogre glares at Bran"), damage numbers, and the enemy's state as text written for that enemy: "The captain is limping", "The shaman's chanting is weakening". The first time you meet an enemy, its card fills in three lines: how it picks its targets, its signature move, and one thing worth knowing. Twists on grudges make up the enemy roster. Cowards go for whoever looks weakest. Proud ones shrug off taunts. A pack shares one grudge. A fickle one forgets its grudge every few rounds. A jealous one hates healers. Each has several answers.

**A fight.** A bandit captain, who is proud, and two crossbowmen, who are cowards. Your party: Bran with his shield, Wren the cutpurse, Ysolde the storm witch, and Father Odo.
- In round 1 the blows fall at random. Bran's challenge bounces off the proud captain, and Wren's first stab makes him glare at her.
- You call Steady. Wren eases off, and Bran's heavy blows pile up the grudge until the captain turns to him. Now every stab of Wren's is a blindside.
- The crossbowmen keep shooting Ysolde, who looks the weakest, and Odo's heals keep her standing.
- One crossbowman drops, you call All-out, and the captain falls fast.
- Odo has spent half his MP, with two fights left before the boss.

**What I like.** Every fight has one thing to watch, which is your "who takes aggro when". Jobs come out of one rule rather than being handed out, so odd builds work: a caster who tanks, or a healer so gentle she's never noticed. Enemy gimmicks are twists on the same rule, so none has only one answer.

**What worries me.** It's shaped like an MMO, and could pull every party into the same tank, healer and two strikers. The enemies that break that shape have to carry a lot. And grudges are hidden numbers, so a switch of target can feel sudden: the feed needs a warning first, like "Its eyes flick to Ysolde".

## Hearts

**The idea.** Your units aren't tools. They're characters, and they choose for themselves. Each turn a unit looks at what it could do and how much each option appeals right now. Saving an ally on the brink appeals a lot, finishing a wounded foe appeals, and a buff that's already up appeals not at all. Two things make each unit its own:

- **Temperament** tilts what appeals. The Bold like big hits on big targets. The Kind like healing and shielding. The Sly like weakening and finishing. The Steady like sure things. The Wild like whatever's biggest.
- **Wits** decide how often a unit takes the best option rather than one that merely appeals. A clever unit is reliable. A dim one surprises you, for better and worse.

Learning your party is learning its people: the "learn your party by trying it" from your SaGa and Dragon Warrior Monsters notes.

**How a fight runs.** Rounds: fastest first, and exact ties at random. Picking a target is part of each unit's judgement: clever units go for the foe that matters, and dim ones swing at whatever's nearest. Enemies choose the same way, with temperaments of their own. A cunning witch goes for your healer, and a dim ogre swings at whoever's closest.

**Momentum.** One rope between the two sides. It moves on the big moments, not on chip damage: a kill, a fall, a critical hit, a rescue at the brink. It changes no numbers. It changes hearts: the Bold get bolder when you're ahead, the Timid get shaky when you're behind, and the Steady don't care. Enemies with weak nerve break when the rope reaches their end, and a broken band scatters. You can win by breaking them, not only by killing them all.

**Stats.** Seven:

- **Health.**
- **Might:** damage with weapons.
- **Will:** damage with spells, and healing.
- **Guard:** taken off every weapon hit.
- **Speed:** who acts first in a round.
- **Wits:** how good a unit's judgement is.
- **MP:** pays for spells, and lasts the whole run. Clever units spend it sensibly, and dim ones waste it on fights that didn't need it.

Every hit has a small chance to be critical, as in Dragon Quest. Crits matter more here than in the other sketches, because they swing momentum. Temperament isn't a stat: it comes with the unit, and rare gear might change it.

**Your hand in it: attitudes.** Each unit holds an attitude for long stretches: Fierce, Even or Wary. An attitude adds its own tilt on top of the unit's temperament. It isn't an order:

- A Kind herbalist on Fierce still saves someone about to die, because nothing appeals more. But she stops topping people up and hits with her staff instead.
- A Bold axeman on Wary still swings most turns, but now he stands over the herbalist when goblins come near.

You change an attitude when the fight turns, not turn by turn. How far it bends a unit depends on the unit: a Steady unit bends easily, and a Wild one barely listens.

**Luck.** It lives in judgement. Dim and Wild units, yours and the enemy's, make surprising picks. That's a build choice, though: a clever party is reliable, and bringing a brilliant but dim berserker is choosing chaos. Crits add the rest.

**How it ends.** By health, or by nerve. Healing costs MP from a pool that has to last the run, and enemy bands break when momentum turns hard against them.

**Reading it.** The feed gives the reason with the act, in a few words: "Hrolf (Bold) charges the shaman." The enemy's state is text in its own voice: "The goblins are wavering." An enemy's card fills in three lines: its temperament, its signature move, and a habit.

**A fight.** A goblin warband, which is Timid, and its shaman, who is Sly. Your party: Hrolf, Bold and dim, with a great axe; Marta, a Kind and clever herbalist; Sable, a Sly duelist; and Ilse, a Steady shieldmaiden. Everyone starts on Even.
- Hrolf crits a goblin and kills it. Momentum swings your way, Hrolf gets bolder, and the goblins falter.
- The shaman's firebomb drops Sable, and the rope swings back. The goblins rally and rush Marta.
- You set Hrolf to Wary. He's still Bold, so he still swings, but now at whatever is on Marta. Ilse holds.
- Two more kills, and the warband breaks and scatters, leaving the shaman alone.
- Leave Hrolf on Fierce instead, and he chases the shaman across the field while Marta goes down.

**What I like.** Units feel like people: the personality you called really cool, and weren't sure fits. Attitudes are incentives, not orders, which is how you described Dragon Warrior Monsters. Momentum makes the ebb and flow visible, so you can see when to change an attitude, and it gives comebacks and routs a shape.

**What worries me.** It's the hardest of the three to read: "why did he do that?" always needs an answer, and the feed has only a few words to give it. It's also the hardest to simulate, because the judgement has to be written well or the units will look stupid. And a dim unit's bad pick can lose a fight, which you said shouldn't feel like bad luck. It's defensible only because you brought that unit knowing it's dim.

## The Ladder

**The idea.** Every hit knocks some footing out from under its target. Weapons knock more than spells, and heavy weapons the most. When a unit's footing runs out, it **staggers**: it loses its next turn and takes extra damage. A few moves only work on a staggered target, and knock it **down**: it loses more turns, and its guard drops. A few moves **crush** a downed target for huge damage. That's one ladder, the same for everyone: STAGGER, DOWN, CRUSH. You build a party that climbs it, and enemies climb it against you: an ogre's club can stagger your healer just before her heal.

**How a fight runs.** Rounds: fastest first, and exact ties at random. Footing recovers a little every turn, so the ladder only climbs when hits land fast on one target. By default your units pick their targets at random, so their attacks spread. A unit's card can give it a habit, such as a fencer who duels the biggest foe, or a sapper who hunts downed ones. Habits are how you aim the machine. Reactions hang off the ladder, like a duelist who lunges whenever an ally staggers something. I'd keep chains short and punchy as a design aim, not a hard cap.

**Stats.** Six:

- **Health.**
- **Power:** all damage. Guard reduces blades but not spells, which knock little footing.
- **Guard:** taken off every weapon hit.
- **Speed:** who acts first in a round.
- **Impact:** how much footing each hit knocks.
- **Footing:** how much it takes to stagger you.

Impact against Footing is a threshold. Below it, a unit's hits barely rock the target; above it, the target staggers in a round. That's your off-build that suddenly works. There are no random crits: CRUSH is the big hit, and you earn it. There's no dodging either. Some foes are just hard to knock down.

**Your hand in it.** None. The strategy is the party, and the fun is watching the machine you built run, or seeing exactly where it jams. This sketch tests a question about the first pass: did Classic feel bare because it had no input, or because it had no machine?

**Luck.** Only in targeting, and in which move an enemy picks. Spread-out hits stall the ladder, so a pack of small foes is a real problem for a party built to climb one big one.

**How it ends.** By health. A downed enemy can't act, so a climbing ladder ends stalemates: a boss that heals itself can't heal while it's staggered.

**Reading it.** Big words (STAGGER, DOWN, CRUSH), and footing as text: "The sentinel sways." An enemy's card fills in three lines: how hard it is to stagger, what happens when you do, and its signature move. Enemy gimmicks are twists on footing. One can't be knocked down. One floats and has no footing at all. One recovers almost at once. One answers a stagger with a counter.

**A fight.** A stone sentinel, whose footing is huge and recovers fast, and two lantern wisps, which float and can't be staggered. Your party: two fencers; Dagny the shieldbreaker, whose bash knocks staggered foes down; and Old Tom, a sapper with a powder keg.
- For two rounds the fencers' hits split between the wisps and the sentinel. The sentinel recovers, and nothing climbs while the wisps burn Tom.
- A fencer kills a wisp, and now the hits land on one target. STAGGER. Dagny's bash: DOWN. Tom's keg: CRUSH, for most of the sentinel's health.
- Next try, you swap a fencer for a lamplighter who snuffs wisps, and the machine runs from round 2.

**What I like.** It's the Siralim pleasure of planning a machine and watching it work, with one shared ladder instead of a hundred triggers. Thresholds are everywhere. Every enemy gimmick is a twist on footing, so each has several answers. And the big words suit the Game Boy look.

**What worries me.** Units stop being self-contained. The concept spec says abilities don't chain across classes, and here a fencer sets up a sapper. I think it stays simulatable, because everything goes through one shared ladder rather than pairs of abilities, but it's a challenge to the concept, and yours to accept. Parties could also settle into a formula (someone to stagger, someone to knock down, someone to crush), so it needs units that climb two rungs, and enemies with no footing. And there's no input, which you value.

## My lean

Grudges, if I had to build one tomorrow. Every fight has a spine you can follow from the first round, its call has two real tensions in it (pulling the boss off the tank, and spending MP the boss will need), and its enemy gimmicks write themselves. I'd want to borrow the Ladder's footing for heavy hitters, and Hearts' temperaments for enemies. Hearts is the one I find most charming, and the one I'm least sure can be made readable.
