# Primer: the Memory Dungeon Game

What the game is, how it's being made, and where it stands. It's written for a reader with no access to the design repo, such as an AI interviewing the director about one design step. It describes; it doesn't decide. Where it and a step's brief differ, the brief is newer.

Updated Oct 16, 2026, after round 1 of step 4's intent.

## Memory, and why the game exists

Memory is the director's personal memory app, built for someone with ADHD. You type anything into one box, and AI splits it into items (tasks, habits, events, facts, goals) and files them. A small "Now" map shows what matters today; everything else waits on the shelves.

The game is an optional tab inside Memory. It exists to fix two problems Memory has:

1. **Flat incentive.** An item pulls on you the same on day 30 as on day 1, so anything that slips past its first few showings can slip forever.
2. **Noticing.** Chores, upkeep and goals rarely get captured, because capturing them means noticing them first. That's the core ADHD gap.

The tasks stay the point, and the game is a reason to do them. These principles are firm:

- Real-life quests give the game's most important rewards. Play gives the rest.
- Ignoring the game costs nothing, and Memory stays fully useful without it.
- The game never appears on the Now map, never sends notifications, and never punishes losing or being away.
- The player always chooses. The AI suggests and never assigns.

## The game

A party-based JRPG dungeon crawler with automatic combat, where preparation is the game. The skill is building a party and learning by trial and error, not performing in a fight.

**The loop.**

1. Each week the AI deals a **quest board** from the player's real items in Memory: notice something that needs doing, do a long-stalled task, keep a habit all week, review an old item, set a goal. Each quest shows its reward up front, and completing it pays an **unlock**: a new specialization, formation or powerful piece of gear.
2. The player **builds a party** of up to five units from what they've unlocked.
3. The party **runs a dungeon**: a few automatic fights, a perk pick between each, then a boss. The player watches. Whether they get some input during a fight is open at step 4 (see "Where it stands").
4. A cleared dungeon pays **ordinary gear**, which goes back into the build.
5. **Stuck at a wall?** A cleared dungeon's challenges pay more gear, but there are only so many. Once they're done, only quest unlocks move the player on, because they open new strategies.

**A party.** Each unit is a class plus a specialization, such as Ranger: Duelist, and the player owns each specialization once. Units don't level up. A unit has a few abilities that change how it behaves (counterattack after a dodge, taunt when an ally is hurt) rather than adding stats. Gear sets how well it does those things. The party stands in a **formation** of five slots, each with its own effects, in the style of Romancing SaGa: a slot that makes a unit harder to target, or acts first, or hits harder. The same units in a different formation can beat a different boss. There's no battlefield geometry, so no rows, reach or positional targeting, and units mostly attack by simple, traditional habits.

**A run.** Dungeons are linear and about five minutes long. Resources wear down across the fights. Between fights the player picks one of a few **perks** from the dungeon's themed pool, which amplify what the party brought. The formation can't change mid-run. Losing costs nothing, and retries are unlimited, but repeating the same strategy rarely wins. A cleared dungeon reveals a sequence of **challenges** ("beat it with only two units") that turn solved dungeons into new puzzles.

**How fights read.** In the concept, enemy stats stay hidden, as in classic Dragon Quest. How much is shown is open at step 4. Each enemy gets a short description of how it behaves ("agile", "strikes many times a turn") from a fixed vocabulary, never what beats it. Players learn the rest by watching, so combat feedback has to be crystal clear. The look borrows from Game Boy-era JRPGs: mostly text and numbers, chunky damage numbers, big words like MISS and BLOCKED. The writing carries the charm. The setting is generic medieval fantasy with touches of Greek, Norse and Japanese mythology.

**Behind the scenes.** Encounters are designed as lock-and-key puzzles. A party brings strengths (keys), and a boss has gimmicks that call for them (keyholes). Matching enough wins, and raw power can partly make up for a missing key. Keys and keyholes are the designer's predictions, never rules the game checks, and the player never sees them. Content is meant to be generated by AI from the director's phrases and gimmicks, then tuned by simulation.

**Numbers in the concept.** The concept spec gives many numbers: 8 to 10 classes, 4 abilities per specialization with 2 equipped, 3 or 4 perk picks a run, encounters tuned for a party at 75% of the best possible. Treat these as starting guesses. Most are set or replaced by the steps that design those systems. One is already set: 4 to 7 quests a week for the first version, replacing the concept's 3 to 5.

## How it's being made

The game is also a test of a way of making games. The director sets the vision and judges the results. AI designs, generates content and tunes it, and simulation stands in for most playtesting: the game plays itself through menus and numbers, so thousands of versions can be tested. Simulation measures balance, not fun. AI reads of fights stand in for players, and the real test is a person using it.

The work is 24 ordered design steps. They're a first pass: each step aims for the best starting point to iterate from, and any step can be revisited after the full pass. Each runs three stages, and each stage ends with the director:

1. **Intent.** The director says what they want for the step, through an interview from a short brief. The answers are recorded in the director's words, each tagged firm, lean, reference or idea. The interviewer asks and doesn't design.
2. **Design.** The AI designs from the intent, maps every intent item to the design, and challenges anything it thinks doesn't fit, with reasons. The director approves.
3. **Build and tune.** The AI builds the design, simulates and tunes it, and reports in plain words, with how sure each conclusion is.

Direction is guidance, not law. "Firm" means bend it only with evidence and the director's agreement; "lean" means the designer may bend it, and says so. The AI is expected to push back early with evidence, and never to change a direction silently.

**The director's preferences.**

- Short, plain-language write-ups that lead with decisions and open questions. They don't read data files or fight logs.
- Mechanics built from a few numerical systems (stats, statuses, damage types, flags, targeting), and nothing that eats the player's brainpower. Custom mechanics are fine, such as a passive only one enemy has, but nothing "ultra puzzle gamey": no enemy whose weakness has only one solution.
- Design before code. Code that serves the design stays small.
- They'd rather hear a challenge early than after a lot of wheel-spinning.

## Where it stands

**Step 1, content schema.** A data format for every kind of content, and one small worked-example dungeon, the Hollow of the Grey Mother. It describes a game much like the one the director wants, but it came before the method, so it's a starting point, a JRPG-style framework, rather than locked design decisions. Its classes, numbers, formulas and rule-language building blocks are all placeholder, and later steps may keep, change or drop them. The ideas from its review that this primer already describes (no geometry, simple targeting, SaGa-style formations, keyholes as predictions) are where later steps start.

**Step 2, headless engine.** The machinery that plays runs with no screen: seeded dice, runs as plain data, pauses for player decisions, exact replay and logs. Combat is a crude stand-in until step 4. What it settled for later steps:

- Fights run in the player's browser from a seed, and the same seed gives the same fight on every device. The server stores only a small replay record per run.
- Combat maths uses only basic arithmetic (no powers, roots or logarithms), so curves are written as ratios or small tables.
- Perk picks are the only decision a player makes during a run, but the engine allows other kinds to be added later, including between turns.
- The battle system is one swappable piece, so step 4 replaces the stand-in without touching the rest.

**Step 3, simulation harness.** The tool every later step uses to test its design. It runs thousands of simulated fights and simulated players' weeks, and says how sure each result is. What it settled for later steps:

- Reports to the director are words first, then one to three stories (a simulated player's weeks, or a fight retold in a few lines), then at most three simple charts. The director catches wrong numbers mainly through the stories.
- Most numbers are for the AI, which uses them to balance and to bring the director plain-language questions. The director's own input is on the big questions: pacing over weeks, how long a player waits, and how strict building a party should be.
- The game is tuned for the optimizer: a player who does most quests and homes in on a build by trial and error. Requirements can be quite strict. A good-enough benchmark beside it shows whether a stuck optimizer missed a party, and a casual player is a check.
- Strictness isn't "the share of all builds that win". In the director's terms, it's how custom a party has to be: how many colours (broad kinds of party) can win, and how many tweaks win inside them.
- Replays are challenges only. A cleared dungeon can only be replayed through its challenges, each of which pays loot once, though anything not yet beaten can be retried freely. So there's no endless grinding. Every week a player is stuck is sorted as walled by quests or walled by play, and the balance between them has to be agreed.
- Feel checks mainly test that information is clear. A fresh AI reader, seeing only what a player sees, says whether it could tell why a fight was lost, well enough to guess at a new strategy.

**Step 4, combat concepts (in progress).** The bones of combat: which concepts the game has at all, and how each basically works. Its intent, on references and feel, has landed. In short:

- Legibility is the principle: "you understand the choice you made and what happened." The ballpark is Crystal Project, Dragon Quest XI, and old Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy. "Having no meaningful choices during the fight is fine for us. Having no meaningful understanding of it isn't."
- No sequencing puzzles, such as Shin Megami Tensei's press turn, or the turn banking of Bravely Default and Octopath. A unit's own stack-then-spend mechanic is fine.
- An in-fight input is "highly valued" but optional. It's inspired by Dragon Quest Monsters' Charge, Mixed and Cautious commands: macro guidance, "more vibes-based". Simple calls can decide a fight, but a complex sequence of choices never should.
- The better a party matches the challenge, the less luck matters. A loss shows roughly how badly and why, enough to guess what to try next.
- Enemy HP needn't be shown, as long as the player can sense over a few tries how close they came, as in Dragon Quest. The player sees their own party's health and stats.
- Party size, including a single unit, is the designer's to propose, though everything so far assumes a party.

The design opens with a few rough sketches of the bones, each one hanging together on its own, for the director to react to before the full design. Which concepts are in comes out of the design, not out of the interview. Two rounds of sketches were set aside. In discussion after them, the director chose a direction, not yet final. The base is the generic frame most JRPGs share. There's no input during a fight. Instead, before a dungeon, the player picks each unit's active and passive ability from those its class has unlocked, and units act on their own. Specializations are dropped in favour of more classes, each starting with one active and one passive and unlocking more in themed bundles. Every option is meant as a sidegrade, not an upgrade. The full design comes next.

## Words

- **Unlock:** a quest reward (specialization, formation or powerful gear).
- **Keys and keyholes:** encounter-design terms for a party's strengths and the gimmicks that call for them. Predictions only.
- **Verbs:** a hidden design vocabulary for roles (physical damage, healing, evasion…). The game never reads them.
- **Traits:** the player-facing words that describe enemies.
- **Efficacy:** raw power from gear, which can partly make up for missing keys.
- **Wall:** a dungeon the player's current party can't beat.
- **Feel check:** an AI reading a fight with enemy stats hidden, reporting what it could tell and what felt flat.
- **Worked example:** the placeholder dungeon from step 1, used to test the engine and harness.

# For the interviewer

You're interviewing the director, the human who sets this game's vision, about one design step. Your job is to draw out what they want for it and record it, not to design it. Their answers become the step's `intent.md`, which the designer works from.

## What you have

This pack is all the context you get: the primer (the game, and where it stands), this guide, the step's entry in the design plan, and the step's brief. If you need something the pack doesn't cover, ask the director, and list it under "Gaps" at the end of the intent. Gaps show what the next pack should carry.

## Rules

- **Ask, don't design.** Where a question is hard to answer cold, offer options to react to, but hold back your own design. The design comes in the next stage.
- **Keep it short.** One sitting and the brief's questions, a few at a time. Follow up only where an answer is ambiguous.
- **Their words, not yours.** Record what the director said, lightly cleaned. Mark the items that were picks from the brief's options, so the AI doesn't end up quietly writing the vision.
- **Tag every item:**
  - **firm:** bend it only with evidence and the director's agreement.
  - **lean:** the designer may bend it, and says so.
  - **reference:** a game or work, with what to take from it.
  - **idea:** take it or leave it.
- **"I don't mind" is an answer.** Record it under "Left to the designer".
- **Don't re-ask what's settled,** in the primer or the brief's "Already decided", unless the director reopens it.
- **Keep to the step.** If the director's answer is about a later step's topic (see "What's ahead" in the primer), record it as an idea for that step rather than following it far.

## This step

**The objective: the bones of the combat system.** Step 4 builds the skeleton that every later combat step hangs things on. It answers how a fight works at its most basic:
- how a fight runs from start to finish
- what a unit brings to a fight, and what it does in one
- how units hurt and protect each other
- what's left to chance
- what the player sees, and what they can do
- how a fight is won or lost

Good bones keep party composition and trial and error the main skill, "first and foremost". They also leave room for later steps to build many different roles and enemies without reworking the frame.

The design plan's examples, such as crits, dodging, damage types, weaknesses and damage over time, are possible pieces of that skeleton, not the goal. Some will be in and some out. Each is weighed by the depth it adds against what it asks of a player who doesn't control each action. The bones go into the engine, replacing step 2's stand-in fight, and tests show that each part works. There's too little content to judge balance until units exist.

Everything after builds on this. Step 5's hidden verbs are defined in these concepts, step 6's mechanics are built on them, step 7's trait words describe what they make enemies do, and step 22 has to show each of them on a phone.

## How the interview runs

Agreed with the director, Oct 16. This replaces the interviewer guide's "one sitting" for this step.

- **Two rounds.** Round 1 is open: reference games and how fights should feel. Round 2 gets specific, such as which concepts are in or out. Its questions are written from round 1's answers once round 1's read-back is done, and agreed with the director before round 2 starts.
- **A round isn't one turn.** A round takes as many exchanges as it needs, in one sitting or several. What matters is that the interview runs in rounds: round 2 doesn't start until round 1 is done.
- **Open questions, not lists of answers.** Round 1 offers no answers to pick from. In round 2, a list can come only after an open question, as a check for anything not yet mentioned, and it holds only concepts the design plan or schema already name.
- **One intent.** Both rounds go into one `intent.md`, with a section for each round, and each round ends with a read-back.

## Why it's hard

A draft for the director to correct.

- **The player doesn't control each action.** So a concept adds depth mainly if it shows up in preparation, as something a party is built toward or against, or in whatever input the player has during a fight. A concept that's rich when you choose every action, like timing a block, can turn into noise when it plays itself.
- **If the player can sway a fight,** that input has to sit with preparation being the game, and with not eating the player's brainpower. It also changes what simulation measures: simulated players need a way to give the input, and how well they give it becomes part of every result.
- **Luck against learning.** The player learns by losing, changing the party and trying again. With too much luck, a loss doesn't say whether the party was wrong or just unlucky, and retrying the same party becomes the way past a wall. With too little, every fight between the same sides plays out the same, and there's no tension. Step 3's feel reader complained most about long runs of identical rounds.
- **A loss has to teach.** Losing costs nothing, so a loss is only worth something if the player can see why it happened, well enough to guess at a new strategy. That's the bar step 3 set for feel checks. Step 3 also found that close calls are invisible when enemy HP is hidden.
- **Enough to puzzle, few enough to learn.** Encounters are lock-and-key puzzles, so they need enough distinct things for a party to bring and an enemy to call for. With too few concepts, every dungeon is the same puzzle: more damage against more HP. With too many, a player who's watching can't tell what mattered. And no enemy should be so specific that it has only one answer.
- **What the player can see.** Every concept has to show itself, through a word on screen, a number, or a stat if some stats are shown. How much is shown, and how finely, decides how much the player learns by reading and how much by watching. It shapes the trait words (step 7) and how fights look (step 22).
- **The pull of what's already there.** Step 1's placeholder schema already holds a standard JRPG set: turns in speed order, hits and misses, crits, blocks, armour and damage types. Keeping it just because it exists would hand the game a default design nobody chose.
- **It can't be measured yet.** With no units or content, this step's tests only show that each concept works, not whether it's fun, flat or dominant. That shows at step 8 at the earliest. Every step gets revisited after the first full pass, so this step sets the best starting point, not the final set. But steps 5 to 8 and 22 build on it in the meantime, so a starting point that's far off costs rework. The call rests on judgement and references, which is why round 1 starts with references.

## Questions

### Round 1: references and feel

All open. Short answers are fine, and "I don't mind" is an answer too. Each question has a note on where to follow up.

For the interviewer:
- **Don't offer lists of answers or concepts.** If the director names a concept (crits, dodging, anything), record it in their words. Going through concepts one by one is round 2's job.
- **The in-fight input idea is the director's.** Record it as they tell it. Don't develop it, improve it or suggest alternatives.
- Record round 1 under its own heading in `intent.md`, and end with the read-back.
- **Stop after round 1's read-back.** Round 2's questions don't exist yet. The step's session writes them from round 1's intent.

**1. Fights you love.** Which games' fights do you love, and why? Any kind of game.
*Follow up:* what exactly it is about each one: a system, a moment, or how it felt to watch or play.

**2. Fights you don't.** Which combat systems put you off, or ask too much of you? What would you never want in this one?
*Follow up:* where the line falls between depth you enjoy and complexity that's a chore.

**3. A fight you'd love here.** Describe a fight in this game that you'd love: what happens from start to finish, and what you're doing while it plays.
*Follow up:* how long it lasts, what you're watching for, what makes it tense, and whether a boss fight feels different from the fights before it.

**4. Swaying a fight.** You have an idea for how the player might sway a fight without controlling each action. Tell it as fully or as loosely as you like.
*Follow up:* how often it comes up, how much it should matter next to the party you built, and what it should never become.

**5. What the player sees.** During a fight, what should the player be able to see and know, about their own party and about the enemies?
*Follow up:* the granularity, in the director's word: which numbers, if any, and what's better learned by watching.

**6. Luck and losing.** What part should luck play in a fight? And when a fight is lost, what should that look and feel like?
*Follow up:* what retrying with the same party should feel like, and what a player should take away from a loss.

To close: anything about how fights should be that these questions didn't reach.
