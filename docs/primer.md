# Primer: the Memory Dungeon Game

What the game is, how it's being made, and where it stands. It's written for a reader with no access to the design repo, such as an AI interviewing the director about one design step. It describes; it doesn't decide. Where it and a step's brief differ, the brief is newer.

Updated Oct 29, 2026, after the roster, the gear's design and the cleanup that brought the base game to two kinds of damage and four gear slots.

## Memory, and why the game exists

Memory is the director's personal memory app, built for someone with ADHD. You type anything into one box, and AI splits it into items (tasks, habits, events, facts, goals) and files them. A small "Now" map shows what matters today; everything else waits on the shelves.

The game is an optional tab inside Memory. It exists to fix two problems Memory has:

1. **Flat incentive.** An item pulls on you the same on day 30 as on day 1, so anything that slips past its first few showings can slip forever.
2. **Noticing.** Chores, upkeep and goals rarely get captured, because capturing them means noticing them first. That's the core ADHD gap.

The tasks stay the point, and the game is a reason to do them. These principles are firm:

- Real-life quests give the game's most important rewards. Play gives the rest.
- Ignoring the game costs nothing, and Memory stays fully useful without it.
- The game never appears on the Now map, never sends notifications, and never punishes losing or being away.
- The player always chooses. The AI suggests and never assigns.

## The game

A party-based JRPG dungeon crawler with automatic combat, where preparation is the game. The skill is building a party and learning by trial and error, not performing in a fight.

**Right now, the core set.** What's being built at the moment is a vertical slice of this game, which the director designed as four documents (rules and vocabulary, classes, gear and formation lists, enemies): the game part, without the quest board or reward streams. Its progression is still to be decided, starting with none: everything is available, and combat, formations, gear and the battle system are tested freeform. The full game described below grows out of it later. Where the slice differs from the concept (parties of 3 rather than 5 in the first playable, 16 classes, four gear slots per unit, formations of three slots), the slice is what's built.

**The loop.**

1. Each week the AI deals a **quest board** from the player's real items in Memory: notice something that needs doing, do a long-stalled task, keep a habit all week, review an old item, set a goal. Each quest shows its reward up front, and completing it pays an **unlock**: a new class, formation or powerful piece of gear.
2. The player **builds a party** of up to five units from what they've unlocked.
3. The party **runs a dungeon**: a few automatic fights, a perk pick between each, then a boss. The player watches.
4. A cleared dungeon pays **ordinary gear**, which goes back into the build.
5. **Stuck at a wall?** A cleared dungeon's challenges pay more gear, but there are only so many. Once they're done, only quest unlocks move the player on, because they open new strategies.

**A party.** Each unit is a class; there are no specializations. The player owns each class once and gears it once, and a party holds at most one of each. To start, a class has a unique passive, five abilities of which it brings three into a fight, and a restriction to some weapon types. Units don't level up. Its abilities change how it behaves (counterattack after a dodge, taunt when an ally is hurt) rather than adding stats. Gear sets how well it does those things. The party stands in a **formation** of five slots, each with its own effects, in the style of Romancing SaGa: a slot that makes a unit harder to target, or acts first, or hits harder. The same units in a different formation can beat a different boss. There's no battlefield geometry, so no rows, reach or positional targeting, and units mostly attack by simple, traditional habits.

**A run.** Dungeons are linear and about five minutes long. Resources wear down across the fights. Between fights the player picks one of a few **perks** from the dungeon's themed pool, which amplify what the party brought. The formation can't change mid-run. Losing costs nothing, and retries are unlimited, but repeating the same strategy rarely wins. A cleared dungeon reveals a sequence of **challenges** ("beat it with only two units") that turn solved dungeons into new puzzles.

**How fights read.** Enemy stats stay hidden, as in classic Dragon Quest. Each enemy gets a short description of how it behaves ("agile", "strikes many times a turn") from a fixed vocabulary, never what beats it. Players learn the rest by watching, so combat feedback has to be crystal clear. The look borrows from Game Boy-era JRPGs: mostly text and numbers, chunky damage numbers, big words like MISS and BLOCKED. The writing carries the charm. The setting is generic medieval fantasy with touches of Greek, Norse and Japanese mythology.

**Behind the scenes.** Encounters are designed as lock-and-key puzzles. A party brings strengths (keys), and a boss has gimmicks that call for them (keyholes). Matching enough wins, and raw power can partly make up for a missing key. Keys and keyholes are the designer's predictions, never rules the game checks, and the player never sees them. Content is meant to be generated by AI from the director's phrases and gimmicks, then tuned by simulation.

**Numbers in the concept.** The concept spec gives many numbers: 8 to 10 classes, 5 abilities per class with 3 equipped, 3 or 4 perk picks a run, encounters tuned for a party at 75% of the best possible. Treat these as starting guesses. Most are set or replaced by the steps that design those systems. One is already set: 4 to 7 quests a week for the first version.

## How it's being made

The game is also a test of a way of making games. The director sets the vision and judges the results. AI designs, generates content and tunes it, and simulation stands in for most playtesting: the game plays itself through menus and numbers, so thousands of versions can be tested. Simulation measures balance, not fun. AI reads of fights stand in for players, and the real test is a person using it.

The work was planned as 24 ordered design steps, a first pass to iterate from. The first three built the foundation and the fourth built combat; then steps 4 to 12 proved too interlocked to design one at a time, and the director designed them together as the core set. The steps after it stand. Each step runs three stages, and each stage ends with the director:

1. **Intent.** The director says what they want for the step, through an interview from a short brief. The answers are recorded in the director's words, each tagged firm, lean, reference or idea. The interviewer asks and doesn't design.
2. **Design.** The AI designs from the intent, maps every intent item to the design, and challenges anything it thinks doesn't fit, with reasons. The director approves.
3. **Build and tune.** The AI builds the design, simulates and tunes it, running the checks the step needs (and asking when unsure), and reports in plain words, with how sure each conclusion is.

Direction is guidance, not law. "Firm" means bend it only with evidence and the director's agreement; "lean" means the designer may bend it, and says so. The AI is expected to push back early with evidence, and never to change a direction silently.

**The director's preferences.**

- Short, plain-language write-ups that lead with decisions and open questions. They don't read data files or fight logs.
- Mechanics built from a few numerical systems (stats, statuses, damage types, flags, targeting). No bespoke keywords acting as locks, and nothing that eats the player's brainpower.
- Design before code. Code that serves the design stays small.
- They'd rather hear a challenge early than after a lot of wheel-spinning.

## Where it stands

**Step 1, content schema.** A data format for every kind of content, and one small worked-example dungeon, the Hollow of the Grey Mother. It describes a game much like the one the director wants, but it came before the method, so it's a starting point, a JRPG-style framework, rather than locked design decisions. Its classes, numbers, formulas and rule-language building blocks are all placeholder, and later steps may keep, change or drop them. The ideas from its review that this primer already describes (no geometry, simple targeting, SaGa-style formations, keyholes as predictions) are where later steps start.

**Step 2, headless engine.** The machinery that plays runs with no screen: seeded dice, runs as plain data, pauses for player decisions, exact replay and logs. Fights run in the player's browser from a seed, and the same seed gives the same fight on every device, so the server stores only a small replay record per run. Combat maths uses only basic arithmetic (no powers, roots or logarithms), so curves are written as ratios or small tables. The battle system is one swappable piece, which step 4 filled with real combat without touching the rest.

**Step 3, simulation harness.** The tool every later step uses to test its design. It runs thousands of simulated fights and simulated players' weeks, and says how sure each result is. Reports to the director are words first, then one to three stories (a fight retold in a few lines, or a simulated player's weeks), then at most three simple charts; the director catches wrong numbers mainly through the stories. The game is tuned for the optimizer, a player who does most quests and homes in on a build by trial and error, with a good-enough benchmark beside it and a casual player as a check. Strictness is how custom a party has to be: how many colours (broad kinds of party) can win, and how many tweaks win inside them. For the full game, replays are challenges only, each paying loot once, so there's no endless grinding. Feel checks mainly test that information is clear: a fresh AI reader, seeing only what a player sees, says whether it could tell why a fight was lost.

**Step 4, combat concepts.** The director wrote this design themselves, and it's now the engine's combat. In short:

- Fights are automatic and run on ticks. Every action (a weapon's basic attack, or an ability) has a cast time, a cooldown and a recovery; a unit starts its first available ability, else its basic attack. Abilities have a short default cast and a recovery about a sword's.
- Damage is physical (meets Evasion and Armor) or magical (meets Magic resist, a percentage, and can't be dodged). Step 4 also had slashing, piercing and blunt, and elements with a resistance each; since Oct 29, 2026 they're gone as mechanics, and an element is only a theme. Every amount states its own scaling, from any stats or none; nothing scales by default, and no stat is tied to a damage type (physical things scaling from Attack is a convention). Boosts, damage-taken modifiers, crits, riders and shields follow one resolution order.
- Everything that happens on an event or a timer is a status: buffs, debuffs, damage over time, stuns and silences (lockouts), auras, and passives' reactions. A status is fixed when applied: the application scales the stacks it adds, from whatever stats it names, and a stack is a stack whoever applied it. Status damage is untyped and unresisted by convention. A reapplied Refresh status replaces the old one. Stacking statuses (Intensity) end below 1 stack.
- Default targeting picks foes weighted by Aggro, which taunts (Forced) and stealth (Forbidden) override; abilities can state their own targeting. (Since Oct 26, 2026, foes pick by draw, a curve on Aggro that keeps every unit between a third and three times an ordinary unit's pull.)
- Content is data built from stats, statuses, actions and targeting rules. The numbers are placeholders.

Writing this step showed that the plan's steps 4 to 12 (verbs, mechanics, traits, units, formations, gear, runs, encounters) interlock too much to design one at a time, which led to the core set.

**The core set.** The director's vertical slice, designed as four documents (Oct 23, 2026). *Rules and vocabulary* has the game rules and the combat system: 22 statuses, 7 special stats and 6 effects, each with a tier (Fundamental, Specialist or Signature), 7 elements with a job each (themes only since Oct 29, 2026), 13 weapon kinds (11 since), and the gear and formation rules. *Classes* has 16 classes, each a passive, three abilities and a plan, with a test fight each and parties of 3 against 12 sample challenges. *Gear and formation lists* has 90 pieces and 10 formations, designed against each class's build forks, with a coverage check. *Enemies, encounters and dungeons* has 29 enemies, 27 encounters and 7 dungeons played in order, built against 61 challenges, and adds a few rules, such as summoning and enemy-only Doom and Bulwark. The core set's rules supersede step 4's design where they differ. **It's built (Oct 23, 2026):** the engine runs the core set's rules, every class, piece of gear, formation, enemy, encounter and dungeon is in the content, and every number on the documents' relative scale has a first value, put in the ballpark by a short tuning pass. The report is with the director; the balance pass by simulation comes after it.

## What's ahead

1. **The core set's sign-off:** the director reads the build's report.
2. **A visual interface,** so the director can see and play the slice before more design. It's in (Oct 24, 2026): a party screen (slots, formation, roster), unit sheets, a bestiary, and a battle screen that shows any fight as it plays and tells it in short English messages, with the director's sprites in colour in a retro JRPG frame, published to GitHub Pages so it runs on a phone. The balance pass by simulation, which tunes the first values, comes after the director has looked at it.
3. **The campaign (Oct 25 and 26, 2026).** Playing the interface, the director found everything-available overwhelming and the slice far too easy, and asked for a game to beat: start with the three starters and the first run, and unlock the rest one node at a time. It is a road of 20 nodes (the seven runs, remixes, and trials that set needs on the party, such as "bring the Vampire and the Bard, pick your third"), each node's difficulty set by simulation against exactly what the player owns there, a numbers pass, and the interface's journey with celebrations for each unlock. Two passes on how fights play followed on the same branch: one on pacing and the value of actions (Oct 25), and one from the director's reactions to it (Oct 26): Bleed in place of Poison (it bursts each time its holder is hit), abilities that start a fight charged by the order they're slotted in, casters that run short of MP where the walls don't, Armor that never blanks a hit, and a campfire offering HP, MP or a fallen member back. The director played it, and the director's last change before taking it to main was Aggro by draw (Aggro centred on 100, picks through a curve, so no unit hides at a 1% chance or soaks every blow). **It joined main on Oct 26, 2026** as the base for the next work; `DECISIONS.md` (Step 5) has what it changed, and the core set's documents are updated to match. Next, proposed: a boss pass and a class pass (the casters, the Vampire and the Bramble knight).
4. **A rogue mode, an experiment (Oct 26, 2026).** The director's hunch, after playing a lot: the core set wants to be a roguelite. A branch of main adds a third mode beside the journey and free play: each run is three acts on Slay the Spire's structure, a map of rooms through a themed region with its boss shown from the start, a leader the player owns plus two drafted companions, abilities learned one at a time, gear in four rarities with legendary pieces that bend a rule and name the classes they're for, a shop, campfires, events, and HP carried from fight to fight. A won run adds its party to the classes a run can start with. Every fight's difficulty is set by simulation, so that a simulated player wins about a fifth of its runs (a first win should be an achievement; heat levels make it harder after that), and twelve AI playtesters in six rounds shaped it. It's waiting on the director's play; nothing of it is decided.
5. **The balance pass (Oct 27, 2026).** The director found the slice almost testable but too poorly balanced to play, and said why: past passes had tuned difficulty by win rates, and the difficulty knobs absorbed what the classes did. They asked for a pass that balances from what happens in fights (who does what share of what) and finds the problems itself. A balance sheet now plays a fixed set of fights from both modes with a spread of parties, reads the logs, and gives every class, ability, status, enemy, piece of gear and formation a line against a written band, from the documents and one principle: every option is a real choice, so nothing is useless, dominated or required, and nothing wins on its own. It found a class that was required (the Vampire, through its self-healing), a formation that was the formation (Caravan, through a free Regen worth two healers), bosses that died before casting their signature move, minions killed before they acted, and a party that regained more MP than it spent; six rounds of numbers at frozen knobs fixed them upstream first (how often units act and what they can afford, then what actions do, then who's worth bringing), and the difficulty was re-tuned in both modes after. Two classes stay passengers (the Bard and the Hexer: a buff or a curse on one unit in a party of three is worth less than a body), which is a design question for the director. Win rates now set difficulty only. The report is with the director.
6. **The combat economy pass (Oct 27, 2026).** Reading the balance pass, the director found its macro view true and its per-fight view missing, and gave a list from playing: fights too dense to breathe, cooldowns and cast times flat across classes, mana that didn't exist for half the roster, statuses as an afterthought, bosses that never cast their moves, and nine classes to change. The pass rebuilt the fight's economy from those: slower, bigger swings; cooldown words and cast times that differ by class; every ability's mana on one ladder; statuses that carry a fifth to two fifths of their appliers' damage; bosses that cast their moves several times a fight; and the class kits the director asked for (a Knight that taunts from the start, a Bard that sings every few ticks and heals a little, a Hexer that burns and shocks every foe, a Vampire that bleeds its own life into an ally). An agent reading fights tick by tick caught what the simulation's averages hid (a Burn that was a nuke, not a ramp), and the engine lost a bug that had let Armor go below zero. The report is with the director.
7. **The feel pass (Oct 27 to 28, 2026).** The balancing continued by feel: after every round of number changes, a fresh agent read rogue fights tick by tick and said what a player would feel. Nine rounds took Burn's cap out, gave bosses behaviours of their own, made Chill a slow that shatters, added potions and tonics after every fight, made cooldowns carry from fight to fight, and found two old bugs (two classes' crits dealt nothing extra; the Cleric's emergency haste never switched off). The last round was the director's own list after playing: pacing back near the original's, with more variety between abilities and weapons. The director played the result, found it works well, and closed the pass for now.
8. **No control in fights, and the roster (Oct 28, 2026).** Asked whether fights with no player input give enough agency, the director decided they do, and that they always will: a team should win or lose as a team, not because someone pressed a button at the right moment, so preparation stays the game. The campaign is retired; the rogue mode is the game. Two pillars came out of it. The first, built now: a roster to rotate. A small bench that grows only by choosing a seat as a reward; parties changed any time outside a fight, usually on a fight's preview; no class regains MP in a fight by itself, every class running dry in two or three fights, and those on the bench regaining half their MP after each fight they sit out, so a party is a build to keep running. Recruits know a random one to three of their abilities, and come from their own map room, as do formations (now with rarities). Loot is planned from the roster's gear seats: about 28 pieces and 12 legendaries a full run, a third of the gear offered for classes not in the roster. Difficulty is now set against a dozen hand-built rosters, each fielding its best three for each fight, rather than against the simulated player. The director played it and called it done.
9. **Builds, gear and the cleanup (Oct 28 and 29, 2026).** The second pillar: builds that synergize, from gear, formations and an upgrade for every ability and passive. Its categories are designed: twelve builds written as ways to play, what a formation can be, and the gear. Gear sits in four slots (weapon, armor, offhand, amulet); weapons come in eleven kinds, each a speed, a damage type and one or two hands; armor comes in four types; ten offhand kinds are each shared by two or three classes; the amulet is open to everyone; and a mythic piece fits one class. On the way the director set a rule for the whole game: two kinds of damage, physical and magical, with statuses never resisted and elements kept only as themes, because statuses are what builds are made of. A short step then brought the base game to those rules with no balancing at all: elements out, damage types set from each class's weapons, today's loot re-sorted into the new slots, and two classes re-themed (the Hexer as a spellblade, the Geomancer as an earth fighter). Next, in order: the pieces themselves (all twelve builds made together), then a balance pass of its own.
10. **The full game,** grown out of the slice: content and balance at volume, progression pacing, the quest board, screens, onboarding, and a review against the principles. The original plan's later steps are the starting point, revisited then.

A question that belongs to later work can be noted and left for it.

## Words

- **Balance sheet:** the pass's tool (`npm run balance`): every option's line against a written band, read from the logs of a fixed set of fights in both modes.
- **Core set:** the vertical slice being built now: rules, combat, classes, gear, formations and enemies, played as a campaign (a road of 20 nodes that unlocks it) or freely.
- **Campaign, node, knob:** the slice's road of 20 runs and trials; each is a node, and each node's two knobs (its regular fights' and its boss's strength) are set by simulation.
- **Unlock:** a quest reward (class, formation or powerful gear).
- **Rogue mode:** the game now (the campaign is retired): one run, three acts, everything gained in the run and lost at its end, except the classes a won run adds as leaders.
- **Roster, bench:** the classes a rogue run has, three fighting and the rest on the bench, which regains MP between fights.
- **Keys and keyholes:** encounter-design terms for a party's strengths and the gimmicks that call for them. Predictions only.
- **Verbs:** a hidden design vocabulary for roles (physical damage, healing, evasion…). The game never reads them.
- **Traits:** the player-facing words that describe enemies.
- **Efficacy:** raw power from gear, which can partly make up for missing keys.
- **Wall:** a dungeon the player's current party can't beat.
- **Feel check:** an AI reading a fight with enemy stats hidden, reporting what it could tell and what felt flat.
- **Worked example:** the placeholder dungeon from step 1, used to test the engine and harness.
