# Step 4 intent

Round 1 was gathered with `/intent 4` in a fresh session, Oct 16, 2026. The director wrote answers to the brief's six questions in one document, then answered three follow-ups (on bursts, party size and losing), and corrected two items at the read-back. They had nothing to add for the closing question. Round 1 offered no options, so nothing here was picked from a list. Round 2 comes next, with questions written from these answers.

The director's framing for their examples: "This is an example of what I mean, not a decision on mechanics." References and examples are there to inspire the designer. They aren't requirements.

## Round 1: references and feel

### The principle

- [firm] Legibility. "There should be a good match between the actions you can take and the results they produce, and both should be legible: you understand the choice you made and what happened."
- [firm] "Most of the skill comes from learning and preparation. Execution is reduced to gauging and directing a clearly observable fight, so you can build outcomes you like."
- [lean] "A fight with enough depth and number crunching that the right party doesn't just easily kill the enemy, but one you can observe and understand who did what." Each party member has a job ("the buffer buffs, the healer heals, the ninja stealths and then bursts"), and you can tell which strategies work against which enemies. "Fights come down to preparation more than execution."
- [lean] "Having no meaningful choices during the fight is fine for us. Having no meaningful understanding of it isn't."

### References, as a spectrum of legibility

- [reference] Crystal Project, Dragon Quest XI, old Dragon Quest and Final Fantasy: "This is the ballpark." You see how much damage you did, misses and resistances, but not whether it was the optimal choice. These are old-school JRPGs "before the burst trend", which the director's follow-up says means the turn-banking systems under "Never", not burst damage. These systems can feel automatic, and the industry pivoted to decision-heavy combat because players spend hundreds of hours fighting. "For our input-light game, that's a sign this style fits."
- [reference] Tactics games (Fire Emblem, Triangle Strategy): the most legible end. One unit at a time, with a preview for every action.
- [reference] Pokémon: every move clearly works or doesn't, but "super effective" means you just chase that feedback. "That's too simple for this game."
- [reference] Siralim Ultimate, AFK Arena: "a cacophony of triggers", the end to avoid.

### Never

- [firm] No sequencing puzzles: "systems where the order of actions matters more than the actions". Examples are press turn in Shin Megami Tensei and Metaphor, and turn banking in Bravely Default, Octopath Traveler and "the new Final Fantasy Resonance". The follow-up made the second one precise. It's a turn system built on banking turns, "doing very little with a turn so that in a future turn, your unit can like literally do any skill that they have 4 or 5 times... manipulating turn intensity as a mechanic". It applies to healing, items and tanking alike, and knowing when to bank and when to spend "is like how you win". It's "a game-wide, player-controlled... fight rhythm" tool. "Both taste and practicality, and I wouldn't want it even if simulation could handle it." It has nothing to do with burst damage. A unit's own mechanic, like an assassin that stacks lethality and then uses it, is fine.
- [reference] SMT's press turn is "still one of the best systems ever, because build-making feeds into it". It just doesn't belong in this game.
- [reference] Micromanagement (Baldur's Gate, XCOM): you control every unit's every move, and the problem space gets so big that it's immediately cheesable. "Good for role-playing, not for fun gameplay."
- [reference] Mixed-up control (Monster Hunter Stories): it asks you to predict and react to the enemy, rock-paper-scissors style, while giving you an ally you can't control. "Those two things don't work together. The prediction itself is either brain-dead or guessing."

### Things the director likes

"Examples, not requirements."

- [reference] Off-builds with a threshold: a melee unit turned magic doesn't work at all, then suddenly gets really good. "The reward is gated behind commitment."
- [reference] Shared rules over character gimmicks (Fire Emblem and Doom Depths, rather than Clair Obscur or Final Fantasy X): "a shared set of parameters you stretch to their limits, like double-dipping on a stat that two weapon types share."
- [reference] Keystones (newer Fire Emblem's close-range archer, Pokémon's Truant): "simple rules, with punctuation strong enough that you rethink how things work, without adding new mechanics."
- [reference] Controlled randomness (old Dragon Quest Monsters, Romancing SaGa): you commit to a team and a formation, then you're somewhat out of control. "It's about building in contingencies and a general strategy, then watching it play out."
- [idea] Learning your party by trying it (the macro loops of SaGa 3 and Dragon Warrior Monsters): not knowing exactly how a party will work until you try it, then learning its tendencies. "I'd like to find room for that here, though I don't think the concept has it right now."

### Party size

- [lean] Party size is the designer's to propose, including a single unit. Everything so far points to a party, and the director doesn't know the right size: "Is it 1? Is it 5? Is it 3?" A single unit "is not off the table, but I'm not saying to like hunt it down." If the designer has "an inspired idea" for it, they can pitch it, with reasons. (From the follow-up.)
- [idea] "Hidden proc chances inside fights would suit a single-unit game better. You're only watching one thing, and there could be a fun kind of gambling in it. Without control, though, it could get very slot-machine-y." A party fits build-making, and a single unit makes fights more legible.

### A fight the director would love

- [idea] The example. It's the second try at a boss with a lot of health that heals itself every turn and hits hard. The first try, with a generic party (mage, tank, healer, damage dealer), died quickly: the boss killed the damage dealer with a big hit, or a few. The second party is two healers, an aggro-pulling tank and a burst assassin. The tank takes the big hits and the healers outheal them. That makes a stalemate, but it doesn't solve the boss's healing. So the assassin stacks some kind of lethality, then lands one or two massive hits that kill the boss before its healing goes off. That party wouldn't work against a boss with several enemies, because its overall damage is low.
- [lean] What the director watches for. In a losing run, "the pattern of who deals damage, who dies when, and who takes aggro when", to understand the flow of the fight and see how a different party could make it easier. With the right party: "watch my strategy play out without flaws."
- [lean] Some unpredictability in what units choose to do is okay. It needn't be literal randomness, though that's fine too as long as it's seeded. It could be things like aggro rules, or "the most classic system, where units attack a random unit if no one is pulling aggro". "What I want is to understand the ebb and flow of the fight."
- [lean] What makes it tense: "understanding the back-and-forth pull of who's winning. If my warrior gets really low, I want to feel, 'Oh shit, will my healers get him healed?'"

### Bosses and the fights before them

- [lean] There's no levelling, so there's no power fantasy of fighting the same enemies over and over toward the next level. Instead, "it's more of a roguelite-style resource attrition." The fights before a boss "test your party's resilience: its ability to avoid damage, mitigate it, heal it, or however you want to deal with it. It's an attrition thing."
- [idea, for steps 11 and 12] There's no rule for what a non-boss enemy is: "I'm not comfortable putting a rule here." Non-boss enemies could be light versions of the boss, fodder, or a totally different gimmick. Some dungeons could have a specific enemy you need to deal with in a specific way, and others easy fights where the party just needs enough sustain. Each dungeon's enemy set is designed with the boss in mind, so the dungeon as a whole is beatable: "if the boss is immune to physical damage, so you need mages to kill it, you can't fill the dungeon only with enemies immune to magic damage."

### Swaying a fight

"This is an inspiration, not a requirement."

- [lean] "Some sort of interactive system is highly valued, but if the design doesn't fit one in, or a better design comes without it, that's okay." It's "one of the more compelling combat systems I've seen for this kind of build game, so it's an avenue worth looking down."
- [reference] The arena battles in Dragon Quest Monsters (Dragon Warrior Monsters). You can't control your monsters directly, only give one of three behaviours. Charge goes all out and attacks. Mixed is mostly buffing and debuffing, maybe some healing and damage. Cautious is healing and defending. How you built each monster makes it better or worse at each behaviour: Mixed on a monster with no buffs probably just does a basic attack. You don't pick every turn, because a Fight button repeats whatever you chose last.
- [idea] Dragon Quest Monsters' personality system. A hot-blooded monster with healing spells, set to Cautious, might not heal the lowest ally, or might heal itself instead of reviving someone, because it isn't good at that role. Together the two systems make each monster "feel unique and full of personality". "I'm not sure personality translates to our game. I'm mentioning it because it's really cool."
- [lean] Why: "I want to watch the build I set up play out. That means the units have to be somewhat autonomous and make some of their own decisions. If those decisions can decide a win or a loss and I have no control at all, that's frustrating." Macro control could be interesting: "giving directions on vibes, with incomplete information about the state of the fight."
- [lean] It helps with repetition. "Our game isn't pure lock and key. It's threshold-based, with many solutions to each problem." The same strategy might fail at 60% efficiency and win at 80%, and the only way to tell which party is stronger is to play and watch. So fights get repeated, and sometimes that feels random: "this time he hit my healer one hit before the heal went off." A way to manipulate the fight makes the player feel more in control of that randomness, and makes repetition more engaging.
- [lean] How often: "Most of the time you'd just click Fight." For much of the game you might keep one setup, like Cautious on the healer and Charge on the damage dealer. The commands let you react in a guiding way. If two units suddenly get very low, you might switch an attacker with a pocket heal to Cautious and hope it pulls it off. You might open with Mixed on the whole party because everyone has buffs, then switch to Charge.
- [lean] How much it matters: "It lets you influence the outcome, but you shouldn't win just by doing it. There's more or less a right way to do it." It ties into building: how well a unit carries out each command depends on how it's built.
- [firm] It should never become "a puzzle-game thing where you have to memorize an order of button presses. It's more macro, more guidance, more vibes-based than that." The director clarified this at the read-back. Any input involves some skill, and it can matter, even decide a win or a loss, but not through a complex series of choices, and it isn't the most important thing a player does to win or lose. Simple calls are fine: "do defense when you're being attacked, do attack when you're being offensive, and you don't do that and you lose, that's okay." This isn't: "if you'd only defended on the third turn and then attacked on the fourth turn and then on the fifth turn defended three times." "We don't want it to be too complex to solve."
- [idea] Forms on the director's mind, "not a list to choose from". The commands needn't be Charge, Mixed and Cautious: there could be two, four or five, with other names. They could be party-wide, through the formation, with an aggressive mode and a healing mode. Each class or specialization could have its own. Or they could be a generic set, like Dragon Quest Monsters'. "Whatever form it takes, it could inspire ideas in both buildmaking and how combat works." The director expects the designer to bring their own proposals.

### What the player sees

"This is mostly up to the designer."

- [firm] The player needs to understand the state of the fight, and whether their strategies worked.
- [firm] At the bare minimum, show the effect of what the player is doing. If they're doing poison damage, they need to see that number. The director clarified this at the read-back: a number against health you can't see is fine, as in Dragon Quest, because you get an idea from how many times you deal it. "You can assume an enemy has a lot of health if you see big numbers a bunch of times, not kill them... oh, I must have like 10,000 health. That kind of stuff is okay."
- [lean] Enemy health doesn't have to be shown. But over repeated plays, the player needs a way to intuit roughly where an enemy stands: "am I close to winning? Was I far from winning?"
- [lean] What exactly to show depends on the combat system: "Whatever the core decision space is, we show what the player needs to make those decisions." Combat should run "mostly on legible actions with legible consequences, so showing the flow of combat is what matters most." The player needs to understand what kinds of solutions could work, so actions need to be legible.
- [reference] The Dragon Quest games: "one of the best examples of that kind of system working well."
- [lean] Enemy stats: "I'm open to any and all of it. I personally love stats, but they have to serve the choices we're asking the player to make."
- [lean] The player's own party: transparency on what's happening to the team, and the units' stats, like their health, or "that my mage has higher intelligence than my warrior". "Even Pokémon lets you look at your Pokémon during a fight." An in-combat inspector showing the current percentage of a magic buff isn't a need.
- [idea, for step 11] A dungeon needn't preview what's in it. Not knowing could give a sense of discovery on the first fight. The director is open on it.

### Luck and losing

- [lean] Luck is part of this. Some of it comes from not controlling the party: unless there are specific aggro rules, units might do somewhat random things. Unit behaviour needn't be a source of randomness, or the only one; crits are another example. "I'm open to whatever amount and form of randomness the designer thinks is right, as long as it helps to evoke the feelings I'm mentioning here."
- [firm] "The better a party matches the challenge, the less luck should matter." A perfectly suited party might beat a boss 99% of the time, and a terrible one 0%. There may be a middle space, around 60/40 or 50/50, where the party is good but not great. The numbers are illustrations. "Luck should feel like a question of 'am I doing the right thing or not?'"
- [firm] It should never feel like "I can't believe I lost because my archer didn't hit the right enemy." Players shouldn't rerun fights hoping the AI does something dramatically different. "Watching should feel like following the flow of the fight with your units, like a story."
- [lean] A loss "needs to give me enough information to be able to know how badly I lost or why I lost. Not perfectly, but just enough to be able to vibes through making decisions as to what the next thing you're going to attempt is." For example: "Damn, my warrior died instantly, so I didn't have enough damage to take this fight down." (From the follow-up and question 3.)
- [lean] The player should also "feel celebrated for a huge success or a near loss or a big comeback". (From the follow-up.)

## Left to the designer

From round 1:

- How long a fight lasts. "It's a consequence of the other systems: the speed, how much input there is, and whether it's a game of lots of tiny hits or a few big ones." It can be measured for practicality once there's a graphical style.
- Whether there's an in-fight input, and if so, how it works, when it's used and what it applies to.
- Party size, including a single unit (see "Party size").
- How smart or optimal units are, and the amount and form of randomness.
- What exactly the player sees, and how the player comes to understand a challenge: seeing a mechanic play out (misses, or armour, so you can tell you're not doing enough damage), a spec sheet saying it's weak to fire, or neither, Dragon Quest style.
- Whether combat resources other than health wear down across a run (shared with step 11).
- The brief's "What the designer will decide" list. The director didn't claim any of it.
