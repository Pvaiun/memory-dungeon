# Defining the web: the report

The director's intent is in `intent.md`: colours in place of builds as the web's poles. This is the agent's answer: a read, a frame and a way of working. Nothing in the game has changed.

## Decisions needed

1. **The frame** (below), with its three rules: a colour isn't a role, a colour isn't an element, and a colour's exclusions come from its opposites.
2. **How many colours.** Five, recommended. Sixteen classes put about three at home in each, enough for a colour to field a party alone (it must be able to, and needn't be best at it). Ten pairs sit near the builds we have, so each build can be a test, and the spare pairs are room for builds nobody designed. Six colours thin every pair to about three class identities; four let nearly every party touch most of the wheel.
3. **Are colours shown to the player?** Yes, recommended, on classes and pieces. The director's case rests on colours being easy to read. A card still never says what it's for, only what it's from: a clear palette, with builds still found.
4. **Who seeds the colours:** the director's phrases for some or all of them, or the agent's candidate wheels cut from the sixteen classes' fantasies, for the director to react to.

**If 1 is ruled, three standing rulings get rewritten:** the builds as the loot's scaffold (*Gear design: the builds*), "built backwards from builds" (*Gear design: philosophy*), and, if 3 is yes, "cards say who can wear a piece, never what it's for".

## The read

The director is right, and the earlier tries failed for a reason. The four lines, the clocks and the threads describe how a party wins and what it's made of. Neither can be a pole, because identity comes from what a thing refuses, and an abstract line refuses nothing: its ends combine with everything (29 of the 40 pairs measured changed nothing). A colour refuses for a reason a player can feel. The missing layer sits between the mechanics and the decks, and Magic's colours are that layer.

## The frame

| Layer | Magic's | Ours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanics | keywords, card types | the threads, the statuses, the stats, the gear kinds |
| Poles | the five colours | the colours, new |
| Signposts | signpost uncommons | the classes: the trunk in a home colour, the three identities leaning to pairs, the versions as the turn |
| Ways to win | aggro, control, ramp… | the rhythm space, the four lines and the clocks, which move here: the designer's, not shown |
| Decks | archetypes, and all the rest | the builds, kept as sample decks and as a test |

- **A class is closer to a Commander than a signpost uncommon.** It's always in the fight, and its kit and passive are a large share of what its unit does. So the classes carry more of a colour than Magic's signposts do, and the wheel can't be drawn apart from them: every draft is checked against the sixteen from the first sketch, without redesigning any.
- **A colour isn't a role.** A protecting colour, a mending colour and a killing colour would make every party one of each, and every run the same shape. Every colour can protect, mend and kill, each its own way.
- **A colour isn't an element.** The elemental classes make fire, frost, storm and stone the obvious cut, and that's four of the nine builds being a status's road again. Colours are temperaments: what a colour values and what it can't stand. Fire can be at home in one and appear in another, read differently: the statuses are our flying.
- **Exclusions come from opposition.** On a wheel, each colour has two neighbours and two opposites, and a colour never does what its opposites value. That's where synergy and opposition belong: between colours, in their flavour, not between mechanics.
- **Clear palette, found builds.** Players pick colours, which is the commitment, as in Magic, and find builds inside them.

## The way of working

One layer at a time, each checked against what exists without changing it.

1. **This step: the frame.** The decisions above. Words only.
2. **The wheel.** Two or three candidate wheels, each colour on one card: what it values and can't stand, the ways to win it's good at, what it can't do (named as the challenges it loses to), its exclusives, and how it reads the shared mechanics. Two checks on paper: every class seated (a home colour, its three identities toward pairs), and each build findable in some pair. The director picks or merges one.
3. **The pie.** The threads against the colours: primary, secondary, never, and how each colour reads a shared thread. Then measure: re-tag the web's kept effects and the core set's pieces by colour, for how much of what exists survives, which colours are thin, and what has no colour (the exotics).
4. **The classes.** Identities and versions pointed at pairs; a kit changes only where a class can't be seated.
5. **Authored gaps resumes,** making loot into the pie's holes in place of the builds'.
6. **The run and the numbers:** colours on cards in the interface, the mana question, recruit offers as colour picks, and simulation that the pairs are viable and a one-colour party isn't the default.

Steps 2 and 3 are cheap and reversible, and they're where the vision is set. Nothing in the game changes before step 4.

## Later, not now

- **The mana question:** what stops a party taking the best of every colour. The likeliest answer is a class's colours deciding what its unit wears, as a Commander's colour identity does; offers already roll by class, so it may cost little. The wheel doesn't need it answered.
- **The run's class picks.** Outside the start's draft, recruits mostly come one at a time. If classes are how a party chooses its colours, the run may need more of those choices.
