## This step

Step 4 chooses what a fight is made of: the concepts combat has at all, and the basic rules for each. The design plan's examples are how turns work, dodging, crits, damage types, weaknesses and damage over time. The step also settles how much luck there is, how much depth suits the game, and what a loss looks like. Each concept is weighed by the depth it adds against what it asks of a player who only watches. The chosen concepts go into the engine, replacing step 2's stand-in fight, and tests show that each one works. There's too little content to judge balance until units exist.

Everything after builds on this. Step 5's hidden verbs are defined in these concepts, step 6's mechanics are built on them, step 7's trait words describe what they make enemies do, and step 22 has to show each of them on a phone.

**Where the line falls** (a draft, for the director to check):

- **Step 4:** which concepts exist, and how each basically works. For example, whether damage over time exists at all, and roughly how it ticks.
- **Step 5:** the role fantasies (a summoner? a gambler?), and the verbs that describe them.
- **Step 6:** the specific statuses, triggers and reactions, and how they time against each other.
- **Step 9:** what formation slots do. **Step 11:** what wears down across a run.

## Already decided

The primer has the game. These bear most on this step, and aren't asked again unless the director reopens them.

- **Fights are automatic.** No manual abilities, no programming the party's tactics, no button mashing. Perk picks between fights are the only decision in a run, though the engine leaves room for others. One manual action per fight is held in reserve, in case fights feel too passive.
- **Preparation is the game.** The skill is building a party and learning by trial and error, not performing in a fight.
- **Units don't level up.** Abilities change behaviour rather than add stats, and units are self-contained: abilities don't chain across classes.
- **Where step 4 starts** (from step 1's review, and open to change): no battlefield geometry, so no rows, reach or positional targeting. Units mostly attack by simple habits, going for whoever draws attention, and a few go for the most wounded. Active abilities fire on their own when ready, each with its own targeting. Formation slots work through ordinary numbers like speed, defence and damage multipliers. Enemies are a plain list, and the two sides needn't work the same way.
- **The player learns by watching.** Enemy stats are hidden, as in Dragon Quest, and enemies carry trait words instead. Feedback has to be crystal clear: chunky damage numbers, big words like MISS and BLOCKED. A concept the player can't see happen can't be learned.
- **Few systems, no locks.** Mechanics map onto a few numerical systems (stats, statuses, damage types, flags, targeting). No bespoke keywords that act as locks, and nothing that eats the player's brainpower.
- **Encounters are lock and key,** as predictions only. The concepts chosen here have to be rich enough for about 15 to 20 verbs, with several ways to counter each enemy gimmick.
- **Losing costs nothing,** retries are unlimited, and repeating the same strategy rarely wins.
- **The schema's rule language is a placeholder.** The director hasn't had input on its building blocks. Step 4 may keep it, change it, drop parts of it or start over.
- **The director's input comes at the start** of designing the battle system, not once it's built (step 2).
- **Engine limits:** basic arithmetic only, so curves are written as ratios or small tables; the same seed gives the same fight on every device; and the battle system is one swappable piece. These shape how rules are written, not which concepts are possible.
